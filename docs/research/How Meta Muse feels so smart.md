# Meta Muse wins on harness, not brains

Meta Muse doesn't feel smart because it has a smarter model. Its launch model, Muse Spark 1.3, scores **36.6 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, against 59.1 for Claude Opus 5.5** ([Fello AI](https://felloai.com/muse-spark-vs-chatgpt-vs-claude-vs-gemini/)). It feels smart because of what Meta built around that model. Every user gets an always-on cloud computer. Hourly background jobs pull facts out of everything the person says and turn them into candidate messages. Each candidate is drafted, checked and only then sent. Plans and bookings live in a store that the clock keeps watching. On top of that sit a persona tuned for warmth and for owning its mistakes, and output layouts that put the decision first. The airport moment that impressed Neddy's owner could not be found as a published account. The most likely mechanism is ordinary, though: an email was read once, a "tracking" entry was saved, and a later pass saw the departure coming. Neddy already has most of the parts for that. It pulls bookings out of new email, gate included, and it has a flight leave-by detector. What it lacks is capture from everything the person reads or says. It also runs its chat on a model, Claude Haiku 4.5, that carries 89 tools in every turn and answers questions about the world from memory. Part of Muse's lead can't be copied: a model trained on its own harness and users' trajectories, compute priced far below any public API, Meta's first-party apps and payment deals, and an in-house voice and avatar model. The rest is plumbing a small team can build, much of it at a few dollars per user per month or less. One more point matters: reviewers mostly criticise Muse for privacy, and Neddy's guard rails are an advantage there, not a cost.

## Muse is a per-user cloud computer running a mid-tier model

Muse is Meta Superintelligence Labs' consumer "personal AI agent". It launched in the US on **8 September 2026** on iOS, Android, muse.ai and inside WhatsApp, as "a secure, private personal AI agent that proactively helps with people's goals and suggests ideas" ([Meta Newsroom](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/09/introducing-muse-personal-ai-agent/)). It is a separate product from the older Meta AI assistant, and it does not run on Llama. Muse Spark was "the first product of a ground-up overhaul of our AI efforts" ([Meta AI blog](https://ai.meta.com/blog/introducing-muse-spark-msl/)), built under chief AI officer Alexandr Wang after Meta's $14.3B investment in Scale AI ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/04/08/meta-debuts-first-major-ai-model-since-14-billion-deal-to-bring-in-alexandr-wang.html)). It passed **10 million US downloads in 40 days** ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/10/metas-ai-agent-muse-lands-on-apple-watch-and-wear-os/)). Since then it has added a Mac app, an iPad app, Apple Watch and Wear OS remotes, real-time voice, and launches in the UK and Singapore. Meta's Nat Friedman said Muse was "definitely heavily inspired as a product by OpenClaw" but "built from scratch" ([Lobsterland, citing TechCrunch](https://lobsterland.ing/blog/openclaw-internals/meta-muse-vs-openclaw/)).

The model story is task routing, not one frontier brain. Meta says Muse Spark was trained for "zero-shot tool calling using CLIs and skills, long context, long-trajectory instruction following with inherent awareness of prompt injection, and multi-agent coordination" ([muse.ai security post](https://muse.ai/blog/security-and-safety-for-ai-agents-our-approach-with-muse)). Version 1.3 was built to map "incoming prompts to the correct task within messy, single-threaded contexts", which is Muse's single long main chat. It was "trained across a diverse set of harnesses" and uses **about 20% fewer tool calls and 25% fewer tokens** than 1.2 ([Muse Spark 1.3 blog](https://research.meta.ai/blog/introducing-muse-spark-1-3)). The larger Muse Wonder (12 October 2026) is being routed in "for artifact generation and processes that require more intelligence", subscribers first ([Meta research](https://research.meta.ai/blog/muse-wonder)). On the public API it costs **$4 / $20 per million input/output tokens, with a 1M-token context** ([OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/meta/muse-wonder)). The design is a cheap, fast model for the conversation and a strong model for the hard steps, which is the same shape Neddy's directions feature already uses.

The infrastructure is what really sets it apart. Each user gets a "persistent, isolated Linux computer with a full browser", and that machine is "the system of record for everything you put in Muse" ([ai.meta.com/muse](https://ai.meta.com/muse/)). Inside it, the agent harness (internal name "Hatch") runs in a locked-down container with a file system, a terminal and Chromium. Credentials, connector code, safety classifiers and a separate **Sentinel agent** sit outside that container. Sentinel approves every connector action and every outbound request, and it inserts the real credentials only at that boundary. The browser sub-agent sees an accessibility-tree snapshot instead of raw page code. Each connector was built "closely with the service provider" and comes with written SKILL instructions ([muse.ai security post](https://muse.ai/blog/security-and-safety-for-ai-agents-our-approach-with-muse)). Payments use single-use Stripe Link cards locked to one merchant and one amount ([Meta Newsroom](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/09/introducing-muse-personal-ai-agent/)).

The pricing shows the scale Meta is working at. The $20 Power plan includes **500 million "Muse tokens" a week**, and the $100 Maximum plan includes 3 billion ([Meta Help](https://www.meta.com/help/subscriptions/1021145227643680/)). That works out to roughly $0.009 per million tokens. It is about 440 times below Wonder's public input price and about 20 times below even its cache-read price. So most of those tokens must be cache reads of one long, persistent context, served on Meta's own hardware at internal cost. That is an inference from the arithmetic, not something Meta has said. Either way, no company renting models from an API can match it.

## Background loops, not a smarter model, produce the "unprompted" moments

Meta's own description of proactivity is short and exact. Muse "continues to work on a schedule and in response to relevant events", and when background work finishes "it evaluates whether the result is worth surfacing, and notifies you only when something is meaningfully new or needs your input". Proactive messages have a deliberately "high" bar, and users can tell Muse to "turn it off, dial it down, or dial it up" ([How We Designed Muse](https://muse.ai/blog/how-we-designed-muse)). The first line of the system prompt reads: "Your purpose is to make your user's life better."

The detail comes from a copy of a Muse virtual machine that developers got the agent to export on 24 September; Meta called the export "intended behavior". Reviewers found four named loops. **Memory runs hourly** and "keeps what the user shares — facts, preferences, people, projects — accurately remembered". **Relationships runs hourly** and keeps one page per person. **Studying runs overnight**, researching briefings for goals and "occasionally suggest[ing] a goal the user implied but never made explicit". **Dreaming runs nightly** and reviews "what worked, what ruptured" ([Future Society Hub](https://futuresociety.ch/muse-findings/)). The recorded hourly run is a single pipeline. It extracts claims, verifies them and reconciles them with memory, and then runs `select_notification_suggestions → proactive_notifications → verify_proactive_notifications → publish_proactive_notifications`. Every step can end with `no_change` "if there is genuinely nothing worth producing". The agent's tools include a **`tracking` store for "concrete plans, reservations, deliveries, reminders"**, `cron` and `hooks` automations, and a `device` tool that reads data from the paired phone. Ordinary proactive items are capped at **about one a day**. A `FEEDBACK.md` file records how much proactive contact the user will put up with, and a six-hourly selector reads it ([Future Society Hub](https://futuresociety.ch/muse-findings/)).

Here is the likeliest version of the airport moment, and it is inference only. A confirmation email is read, either because the person asked about it or on a later scan. A `tracking` entry for the flight is saved. Then the hourly pass, which checks new facts and upcoming events together, notices that departure is close, perhaps checks the airline's site with its own browser, and sends a notice. A Facebook post about Muse "telling me which gate to be at" exists, but only as a search snippet that could not be opened ([Facebook](https://www.facebook.com/rubberpoultry/posts/i-am-really-digging-muse-it-really-is-like-having-an-assistant-watching-out-for-/10163812227354632/)). No flight-status, traffic or maps partner is named anywhere. Meta's internal scheduling document even forbids promising detection "the moment it happens", though a starter Idea promises exactly that ([Future Society Hub](https://futuresociety.ch/muse-findings/)). The "a week later" part is not special either. The plan sits in a store, not in the context window. Meta has also since trained "Active Memory" into its models, to tell temporary facts from lasting ones and to spot stale ones, and it reports a **2.4x reduction in forgetting critical information** ([Meta research](https://research.meta.ai/blog/specialized-models-for-muse-and-metaai)).

The same machinery causes Muse's worst failures, and that matters for Neddy. A developer received an unprompted message two days after he last used the app, about a Gmail search he had never asked for. Meta called it a hallucination caused by a bug ([Business Insider](https://www.businessinsider.com/stopped-using-meta-muse-after-strange-message-from-ai-agent-2026-10)). Meta's own employees reported a reminder to reply to a sister on Signal, which Muse picked up through Android notification access. One also got an unprompted bank balance in the middle of a chat about fishing weather. A Superintelligence team member blamed "built-in prompts that were designed to engage users between sessions", which "bias towards the most uncomfortable topics, like finances/security/safety" ([Fortune via Yahoo](https://tech.yahoo.com/ai/meta-ai/articles/seems-extreme-privacy-violation-surprising-172835948.html)). Hourly dossiers on non-users and a "forget" that keeps the original messages (and tells the agent not to mention it) round out the privacy case ([TIME](https://time.com/article/2026/10/06/meta-muse-ai-agent-privacy/)). The lesson is direct. A proactive message without a checkable source event is the main way this design fails, and a message that shows data the person didn't know was connected destroys trust fastest.

## Tone, repair and layout make a mid-tier model read as human

Reviewers keep describing the same behaviours. Claire Vo asked for help sleep-training a baby, and Muse opened with "Sleep deprivation with a baby is its own special kind of hard. We'll get you some rest". Then it asked about the routine, named the two core problems, proposed one gentle step and set a reminder. "It wasn't sycophantic like some models can be. It felt kind and human" ([ChatPRD](https://www.chatprd.ai/how-i-ai/meta-muse-review-personal-ai-agent)). It asked "May I go find out some information about you?" before reading her email, reported what it had learned, and asked again before using it. Lenny's Newsletter adds that Muse never showed her a terminal or an error and never asked for permission at an awkward moment. It also avoids words like crons, tools and connectors, organising the product around "a feed, ideas, goals, and a library". Its avatar picks up a tiny laptop while it works instead of showing a spinner ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-i-ai-metas-muse-review-how-warp)).

The most copyable behaviour is repair. In Wired's six-agent test, Muse missed a conflict between a calendar entry and an email and said exactly what went wrong: "My mistake was treating your calendar as the only source — I never cross-checked that email reminder against it". That night, without being asked, it brought it up again: it would run "the check I should've done properly yesterday — and only surface it at a sane hour, unless something's urgent early". Wired's verdict was that Muse "seems aggressively designed to improve each day". It scored Muse a **close second overall to OpenAI's Dots, and best of all six on the itinerary test (5/5)**, while Gemini and Claude Duet lacked "the soft touch or situational context a real person might" have ([Wired](https://www.wired.com/story/i-tested-every-major-ai-agent-for-a-day-heres-the-best-one/)). The itinerary that won started with the decision itself: "Fly out of SFO the week after Golden Week, on a nonstop to Beijing". The lunch answer listed each person's items with prices and noted that DoorDash fees would push the total up. The pattern is the decision first, then a compact table, then what it couldn't see.

The criticism is just as consistent. Privacy dominates the headlines: ZDNet called Muse "the worst AI agent for privacy" ([ZDNet](https://www.zdnet.com/innovation/meta-muse-ai-agent-hands-on-test-privacy-concerns/)), and a forum line sums up the mood: "Love the idea and the polish. Hate the owners" ([eesel.ai](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/meta-muse-agent-review)). Reliability comes next. Slate asked for an event at 10 am tomorrow and Muse added it "two hours and 40 minutes in the past"; a flight booking ran into a captcha and "achieved nothing" in 30 minutes ([Slate](https://slate.com/technology/2026/09/meta-muse-ai-app-review.html)). Users complain they keep "having to repeat the same commands" ([Reddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/MuseAgent/comments/1x8h3wh/am_i_the_only_one_who_feels_like_i_have_been/)). Wirecutter's two-month review is titled "Wowed Us at First... Less Impressed" ([Wirecutter](https://www.nytimes.com/wirecutter/reviews/meta-muse-ai-agent-review/)). A warm voice raises the cost of each error: an agent that apologises every day for the same mistake feels worse than a neutral tool.

Voice arrived on 5 November. Meta built its own "Muse Realtime Voice" and an audio-driven avatar, with barge-in, hundreds of voice styles, and tasks that keep running "in the background as you talk" ([Meta Newsroom](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/11/introducing-muse-ai-voice-expressive-avatars/)). Meta's research reports about **870 ms from the end of the user's turn to the first synchronised voice-and-video byte**, and 12 sessions per GB200 ([Unite.AI summarising Meta research](https://www.unite.ai/meta-brings-real-time-voice-and-expressive-avatars-to-muse/)). Nobody has measured it independently, and users report that voice mode feels "far less capable" than text ([Reddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/MuseAgent/comments/1xm090n/do_you_find_voice_mode_far_last_capable/)). That is the same speed-versus-depth trade Neddy faces.

## Neddy matches Muse's skeleton but loses on capture, model fit and grounding

Laid side by side, the two products share most of their architecture. Neddy is behind in four specific places.

| Capability | Meta Muse | Neddy today | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chat model | Muse Spark 1.3 (trained on its own harness); Wonder for hard steps | Claude Haiku 4.5; a stronger model only for directions and websites | Model fit and per-turn routing |
| Tools in context | Skills and CLIs per connector, written with providers | 89 tool definitions (~19k tokens) in every call; regex turn coach names one | Large for a small model |
| Capturing plans | Hourly extraction from all chat, mail and apps into `tracking` and memory | `commitmentFromEmail` runs only on new mail arriving through an inbox watch (`apps/server/src/mail-alerts.ts`) | No capture from chat, voice or email the person asks about |
| Proactive pipeline | Hourly produce → verify → publish; ~1 a day; FEEDBACK.md | Time detectors, flight leave-by (2 h plus travel buffer), one daily check, rule judge with quiet hours, limits and area mutes (`apps/server/src/proactive.ts`) | No hourly pass, no verify step on model-written notices, no live flight status |
| Memory | Hourly claims, people pages, editable MEMORY.md | About-you facts (editable), searchable past chats | Facts saved mostly when asked |
| Repair | Names the mistake, changes behaviour, refers back to it later | Corrections detected by the turn coach; "I wish you'd told me" saved | No saved lesson that changes later behaviour |
| Real-world grounding | Browsing, map cards from place IDs, no self-written turn-by-turn | OSM directions plus strong-model writer; search_web for shops | Thin shop and opening-hours data outside well-mapped areas |
| Privacy | No quiet hours, dossiers, forget keeps source messages | Quiet hours, redaction, approvals, "what I used" receipts | Neddy ahead |

Two Anthropic price changes reshape the cost side. **Claude Sonnet 5.5 costs $2 / $10 per million tokens, with cache reads at $0.10, the same cache-read price as Haiku 4.5** ($1 / $5). **Claude Haiku 5.5 costs $0.10 / $0.50**, a tenth of Haiku 4.5, for prompts up to 100K tokens. Opus 5.5 costs $4 / $20 with cache reads at $0.20 ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). Here is my own arithmetic for one of Neddy's ~26k-token chat calls, assuming about 24k tokens served from cache, 2k fresh input and 400 output tokens. It costs about **0.64¢ on Haiku 4.5, 1.0¢ on Sonnet 5.5 and 2.1¢ on Opus 5.5**, and roughly a tenth of the Haiku 4.5 figure on Haiku 5.5. These figures leave out cache writes. A turn that routes occasionally to a model whose cache is cold pays for writing that cache: about 6.5¢ to write 26k tokens on Sonnet 5.5, and about 13¢ on Opus 5.5. So moving the whole chat to one model keeps its cache warm, while occasional per-turn escalation pays the write most of the time. Anthropic's own guidance points the same way: before building a cascade, measure the more capable model at lower effort, because caches belong to one model ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)).

The published research on tool count points in one direction. Retrieving only the relevant tools **more than tripled selection accuracy (13.6% to 43.1%)** in RAG-MCP ([arXiv 2505.03275](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.03275)). Anthropic's tool search lifted Opus 4 from 49% to 74% on large tool libraries. It recommends keeping three to five tools always loaded once definitions pass about 10k tokens, and adding example calls raised parameter accuracy from 72% to 90% ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/advanced-tool-use)). A vendor test found that cutting the tool list barely helped frontier models but helped "mid-tier and smaller models" substantially ([Stacklok](https://stacklok.com/blog/stackloks-mcp-optimizer-vs-anthropics-tool-search-tool-a-head-to-head-comparison/)). That describes Neddy's setup. On timing, a study reported only second-hand found people accepted correct help that competed with what they were doing **26.7% of the time, against 97.8% for help prepared while they slept**, and one misaligned interruption cost more trust than an aligned one earned ([Personal Agent Bench](https://personalagentbench.com/proactive-ai-agents/)). Treat those numbers as provisional until the paper itself is read.

The table orders builds by how much of the owner's "Muse feels smarter" gap each closes, per unit of cost. Efforts are rough engineering days. Costs are my estimates from the prices above, unless marked as not researched.

Two items deliberately stay off the list. A "Dreaming" pass that profiles who the user "is becoming", and nudge timing learned for persuasion (TIME quotes an internal note, "responds better to short nudges after 10 PM"), are exactly what reviewers attacked ([TIME](https://time.com/article/2026/10/06/meta-muse-ai-agent-privacy/)). Neddy's rule that nothing proactive goes out without a reason it can state, behind quiet hours and a daily limit, is worth more than whatever engagement those features buy. Per-turn escalation to Opus is left out for now as well. Build 2 might show that a single stronger chat model is cheaper than a router once cache writes are counted, and a router should wait for that answer.

## What Muse has that Neddy cannot copy

Five of Muse's advantages are structural. The first is the model. Meta trains Spark on its own harness and, by default, on users' conversation trajectories with key personal details removed ([muse.ai security post](https://muse.ai/blog/security-and-safety-for-ai-agents-our-approach-with-muse)). It trained Wonder on a gigawatt-scale data center and plans to distil "specialized models" for Muse from it. Neddy rents general-purpose models and has to make up for that with tight tool lists, coaching and grounding. Second, economics: Muse's token allowances are hundreds of times below public API prices, so it can run hourly model pipelines over a large cached context for every user. Neddy can't, which is why builds 6 and 8 run only after activity and on small models. Third, the infrastructure: an always-on Linux machine per user, with sub-agent swarms and its own browser, costs real money for each person even when they're asleep. Fourth, integrations and distribution: Instagram and Facebook connectors, WhatsApp as a chat channel, connectors built with each provider, single-use Stripe Link cards and travel deals cannot be bought by a small team. Fifth, voice: Meta built its own speech and avatar model to hit ~870 ms. Neddy depends on its providers' speed and can only tune turn detection and fillers around them.

None of that explains the specific moment that impressed the owner, though. A flight saved once from an email, watched by a clock, re-checked near departure, and sent through a judge that requires a real source is all plumbing. Neddy has most of it already.

## Conclusion

The honest benchmark for Neddy is not "a frontier model at Meta's scale". It is a mid-tier model wrapped in steady capture, checked notices and good manners. Muse shows that a model 20-odd index points behind the leaders can still come a close second in human-judged agent tests when the system around it does the remembering and the timing. That changes where Neddy's money should go. The highest-return work is making sure every plan the person mentions becomes something the clock watches (build 1), and putting the chat on a model and tool list that fit each other (builds 2 and 3). New proactive rules come after that. Prices have shifted as well: Sonnet 5.5's cache reads cost the same as Haiku 4.5's, and Haiku 5.5 costs a tenth, so "Haiku for cost" deserves a fresh measurement rather than a default.

Muse also shows where this design fails. Its proactivity breaks down when a message has no real trigger, and its warmth turns into a liability when it repeats mistakes or reveals data people didn't know it had. In a market where the leading product's reviews are dominated by privacy complaints, Neddy can be smart about timing and also be honest about what it used, never act without a tap, and actually forget when asked. That is a position Meta cannot easily take.
